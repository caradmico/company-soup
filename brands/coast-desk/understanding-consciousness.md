A paper that tries to walk inside Leibniz’s mill — and argue consciousness can be found.

## Purpose

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz:

> “If you could blow the brain up to the size of a mill and walk about inside, you would not find consciousness.”

This paper will attempt to refute this statement.

To refute Leibniz's assertion that one would not find consciousness by examining the brain even if it were the size of a mill, the paper sets Leibniz’s monadology beside contemporary neuroscientific accounts. Leibniz’s monads are indivisible, self-contained units; modern neuroscience treats consciousness as an emergent property of complex neural networks and interactions. Studies on neural oscillations and synchronization illustrate how coordinated activity across different brain regions leads to conscious experience (Llinás et al., 2003; Tononi & Koch, 2021). Empirical data from fMRI and EEG studies show that consciousness correlates with specific patterns of neural activity (Nani et al.; Edlow et al.). The Integrated Information Theory (IIT) posited by Tononi suggests that consciousness arises from the brain's ability to integrate information across different neural networks — a sufficient reason, in the paper’s framing, for why certain neural configurations lead to conscious experience (Tononi & Koch, 2021).

## What is Consciousness

The concept of consciousness has been a subject of philosophical inquiry and scientific investigation for centuries, with numerous definitions reflecting its complex and multifaceted nature.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: Monadology.** Monads are indivisible, dynamic, and fundamental units of reality. According to Leibniz, monads are not influenced by anything external but are driven by their internal principles, with consciousness seen as a property of these monads. This view contrasts sharply with modern neuroscientific perspectives, which consider consciousness as an emergent property of complex neural networks and interactions rather than isolated, self-contained entities.

**David J. Chalmers: The Hard Problem of Consciousness.** Chalmers distinguishes between the "easy" and "hard" problems of consciousness. The "easy" problems involve explaining cognitive functions and neural mechanisms underlying perception, memory, and behavior. The "hard" problem addresses why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experiences, or qualia. A complete understanding of the neural correlates of consciousness does not explain why these processes are accompanied by an experienced internal life.

**John R. Searle: Biological Naturalism.** Searle posits that consciousness is both a real and irreducible biological phenomenon, akin to digestion or photosynthesis. He rejects both dualism, which separates mind and body, and reductive materialism, which attempts to explain consciousness solely in terms of physical processes. He proposes that consciousness arises from neurobiological processes in the brain, maintaining its distinct subjective nature while being an essential aspect of the natural world.

**Max Velmans: Reflexive Model of Consciousness.** Velmans challenges the traditional view that consciousness resides solely within the brain. Conscious experiences result from interactions between the brain and the external world. Perception involves a two-way process where external stimuli are processed by the brain, and conscious experiences project back onto the external world, creating a unified perceptual field.

**Bernard J. Baars: Global Workspace Theory.** Global Workspace Theory (GWT) posits that consciousness functions as a global workspace where information from various unconscious processes is integrated and made available for higher-level cognitive functions. GWT suggests that creating AI systems with a similar global workspace architecture could enable them to exhibit conscious-like behaviors.

**Ned Block: Phenomenal and Access Consciousness.** Phenomenal consciousness refers to the qualitative, experiential aspects of consciousness, often described as qualia. Access consciousness pertains to the cognitive functions that allow information to be used in reasoning, reporting, and guiding behavior. Block argues that these two types of consciousness, though interconnected, require distinct explanations.

**Giulio Tononi and Christof Koch: Integrated Information Theory.** IIT posits that consciousness arises from the brain's ability to integrate information across different neural networks. The level of consciousness corresponds to the degree of informational integration, with more integrated systems exhibiting higher levels of consciousness.

Comparing these definitions highlights the diverse approaches to understanding consciousness. Leibniz's monadology presents a metaphysical perspective, emphasizing isolated, self-contained entities, while Chalmers' "hard problem" and Searle's biological naturalism focus on the subjective experience and its relationship to physical processes. Velmans' reflexive model and Baars' Global Workspace Theory emphasize the integration and interaction of information. Block's distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness addresses different aspects of conscious experience, while Tononi and Koch's IIT offers a measurable, information-based approach.

## Literature overview

Consciousness is a multifaceted phenomenon encompassing awareness, perception, and subjective experience. At its core, consciousness refers to the state of being aware of and able to think about one's own existence, sensations, thoughts, and surroundings. According to Baars et al. (2003), consciousness integrates various cognitive processes, enabling coherent, purposeful behavior and reflective thought. It is often described as the "hard problem" of neuroscience due to the difficulty in explaining how and why subjective experiences arise from neural processes (Chalmers, 2004).

The study of consciousness has deep historical roots. In the 17th century, René Descartes famously proposed the dualistic view of consciousness, distinguishing between the mind and the body as separate entities (Velmans, 1996). The advent of psychology as a scientific discipline in the late 19th and early 20th centuries marked a significant shift: William James' "stream of consciousness" concept emphasized the fluid and continuous nature of conscious experience (Baars, 2002). Behaviorism later dismissed consciousness as a subject of scientific inquiry; the cognitive revolution of the 1950s and 1960s reignited it, integrating insights from psychology, neuroscience, and computer science (Franklin et al., 2000). In recent decades, advances in neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques have provided insights into the neural correlates of consciousness — neuronal oscillations, thalamocortical circuits, and neural synchronization (Llinás et al., 2003; Tononi & Koch, 2021).

If one were to walk around a brain the size of a mill, the paper argues, the observable neural activities — oscillations, synchronization, and reentrant signaling — would indeed represent consciousness.
